Focus

Harmful or useful: the study now centres on harmful fingerprinting

Fingerprinting is harmful when its purpose is to recognise the user for someone other than the service they asked for: advertising, analytics, profiling. It is useful when it serves the service itself: bot and fraud defence, or functionality. That is the ePrivacy “strictly necessary” case. Purpose decides the class. A second axis, tracking capability, ranks scripts and settles the ones whose purpose is unknown. It counts four things: identifying power (a strong technique or ≥3 combined), presence on ≥2 sites, the browser’s own time zone or platform string seen in requests to the vendor, and cookie or storage writes.

Validity

The labels hold up on the checks available without annotators

  • Two independent sources agree moderately. On the 12 domains where both vendor signatures and Tracker Radar give a purpose, they agree on 8 (κ = 0.49). The disagreements are secondary purposes: Stripe fraud against payment, Adobe and HubSpot analytics against advertising.
  • Behaviour matches. 93% of security scripts probe navigator.webdriver, against 68% of ad/analytics scripts (p < 10⁻⁵). 79% of security scripts use a strong technique, against 12% of ad/analytics scripts. These checks use only labels that did not rely on behaviour.
  • Tracker Radar alone is not enough. Only 18 of 59 third-party script domains have a purpose category, and its fingerprinting score is higher for ads (2.35) than for security (1.76).

The two methods cover different sites. The 3 live-confirmed sites stored no consent cookies. The TCF decoder agrees with the live __tcfapi purposes on 20 of 20 visits where both exist. This method follows Matte et al. (S&P 2020), who also decoded stored TC strings.

Finding D

“First-party” fingerprinting is often a hidden vendor

chewy.com, costco.com, nike.com and otto.de all serve their surviving fingerprinting script from the same path, /149e9513-01fa-4fb0-…/. zalando.de and bestbuy.com use randomised path segments. One vendor serving through first-party URLs looks like bot management, which is consistent with FP-Redemption (DIMVA 2021). Your script-domain attribution counts these as first-party, and filter lists cannot block them without breaking the site.

The same systems probably caused many of your 51 HTTP 401/403/429 failures. If so, the excluded sites are the ones that fingerprint most, and prevalence is underestimated.

Finding E

Exfiltration is measurable with a small change

In the baseline, 36 third-party requests on 8 sites carried the browser’s time zone string (Europe/Amsterdam) in the URL. You store request URLs but not bodies. Capturing POST bodies would let you test whether a fingerprinting attribute actually left the browser, and to which domain.

uBlock removed pairs from px-cloud.net (12), forter.com, riskified.com, stripe.network and datadome.co. These are anti-fraud and checkout scripts, so blocking them has a likely breakage cost that you do not measure.

Critique, most serious first

Weaknesses a reviewer will raise

  1. The detector has no measured validity. The thresholds come from Englehardt & Narayanan (2016) and there is no labelled ground truth. Two of the six techniques (WebGL renderer, attribute enumeration) are weak, and they drive most of the blocker effect. The 21 of 22 e-commerce sites flagged are very likely anti-fraud.
  2. An API call is not tracking. Nothing connects a call to an identifier, a network request or a change in what the tracker does. FPTrace (WWW 2025) and FP-tracer (PoPETs 2024) both make that link; your paper should at least discuss them.
  3. The consent verifier was conservative to the point of being wrong. The two bugs above turned the best research question into a negative result. Reject is also combined with GPC, and Privacy Badger turns on GPC by itself, so neither comparison isolates one mechanism.
  4. The sample is shaped by bot detection. Results rest on 93 of 149 sites, one day, a free VPN exit, one subpage and 5 seconds of dwell with no scrolling or clicks. “Beyond the Crawl” (WWW 2025) found crawls miss 45% of the fingerprinting sites real users meet. The browser profile is also inconsistent (German IP, Amsterdam time zone, en-US locale), which can itself trigger bot detection.
  5. Defence cost is missing. The brief asks what protection costs. You measure blocking but not breakage, page load or broken checkout.
  6. Promised work was dropped without explanation. The README lists respawning as RQ4 and respawn.py exists, but the paper says nothing about it.
  7. Known blind spots remain. Workers are not instrumented, CSS-only fingerprinting (“Fashion Faux Pas”, S&P 2023) is invisible to JS hooks, 11 sites have pairs attributed to “(inline/unknown)”, and the hooks can be detected by anything beyond a toString check (Krumnow et al., CoNEXT 2022).
Suggested framing

A sharper paper from the same data

Working title
Does Rejecting Consent Stop Fingerprinting? A Verified-Consent Measurement with Content Blockers
RQ1
How much fingerprinting runs before any consent interaction, and who runs it: ad-tech, analytics, security, or vendors hidden behind first-party URLs?
RQ2
With the stored consent state verified, does rejecting change fingerprinting, compared with its effect on cookies and third-party contacts?
RQ3
Which fingerprinting survives content blockers, by signal strength and by party, and what does blocking cost in breakage?
RQ4
Optional: what share of detected patterns lead to the attribute leaving the browser?

Contributions you could claim:

  • A consent-verification method that combines the live CMP API with offline decoding of stored TC strings and OneTrust cookies. You can show how much a click-only check or an over-strict API check gets wrong.
  • A 2026 EU replication of Papadogiannakis et al. with verified state on both arms. The brief explicitly counts “an existing signal under previously unexplored conditions” as a contribution.
  • A breakdown of what blockers do and do not stop, including vendors hidden behind first-party URLs.
